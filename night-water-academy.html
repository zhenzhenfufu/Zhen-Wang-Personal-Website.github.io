Night Water Academy 夜水书院

First-person Chinese xianxia puzzle game in Unreal Engine 5 · entry to the Tsinghua University School of Architecture × miHoYo digital interactive environment competition · team of five

My part: producer, engine and interaction, art direction, lighting, modelling, and the trailer — everything on the build except the design document.

An expelled student sneaks back into a floating academy at night and finds that the courtyard's reflection no longer matches the courtyard. There is a bridge in the water that is not on the bank. The water keeps the version of the world that somebody edited away.

What I did on it

Five of us made it. One wrote the design — the story, the puzzles, the script. I took everything else that had to exist for it to be a game you can actually launch.

  • Producing. Scope, schedule, what got cut, and keeping five people's work merging into one build rather than five folders.
  • Engine and interaction. The C++ side in Unreal — the implements the player carries, the state each puzzle moves through, the pause and settings layer, and the ink-brush text rendering shared by both levels.
  • Art direction and lighting. The night courtyard look: cool moonlight for shape, a small number of warm lanterns to say where to go, and the colour language the spells use so the player can tell a boundary from a fixed change.
  • Modelling. The architecture and props — moon gate, bridges, the pavilion, the library interior and its coffered ceiling, the shelves and mechanisms of the second level.
  • Video. The trailer and the capture work behind it.

The one rule everything is built on

Objects, registers and archives inside the academy can all be rewritten. Reflections cannot. So the water still shows the bridge that was removed, the pool floor still carries the name that was inked out, and a cup of water still shows the shelves in the order they were first built.

The reflection is never a copy-able answer. You have to find the position and angle where the broken structure under the water lines up with the broken structure above it — and read the difference.

Two levels

  • The floating courtyard. Ring a bell and the reflection of a bridge that does not exist appears in the pool. Take that reflection into a painted scroll, cut it free along its edge by hand, draw it into place with a staff, and fix it with a talisman — now it is a bridge. Then move a lantern until the shadow of a door falls on a sealed wall, and fix that too. The archive inside has its seventeenth entry inked out; the stone at the bottom of the pool does not.
  • The library water-mirror maze. The shelves have been moved and the way out is cut off. Fill a glass at the fountain, set it on one of three stones, and look into it: each stone shows the library in a different state — the one that sank, the one that burned, the one on the night it was finished. Where a half-beam under the water meets the half above it, you can read the three numbers that turn the shelves aside.

How it is built

  • Engine and code. Unreal Engine 5.8.3 with a single C++ module carrying both levels' logic, the shared pause and settings interfaces, and the ink-brush text rendering.
  • Assets by script, not by hand where it counts. Blender plus Python for procedural modelling, UV unwrapping and baking. Interface text is not system type at all — every glyph in use is rendered from calligraphy faces and baked to textures at build time, so the font files never ship. The sound is synthesised in code: ambience, footsteps that switch by ground surface, bells, stone, shelves.
  • Rendering. Lumen global illumination and reflections with hardware ray tracing, Nanite geometry, virtual shadow maps; ray-traced shadows reserved for the moonlight, and distant scenery dropped from ray tracing past a cutoff so the far view costs nothing it does not have to.
  • The cup. Looking into the glass renders a second version of the library through a scene capture into a half-resolution floating-point target, with Lumen and ray tracing switched off inside the capture, re-captured per frame while you turn and throttled down to a fixed rate when you hold still.

Proving it works

A puzzle game fails in a specific way: a player gets somewhere the designer did not intend, or a mechanic stops responding after a reload, and neither shows up in a screenshot. So the build is checked by automated passes rather than by replaying it: every step of each level's main line, picking an object up and putting it back, leaving a puzzle early, restarting mid-level, garbage collection between stages, and a route check that confirms there is no way across the water that skips the bridge puzzle. The packaged build is run start to finish, cold and warm, and the rendering is compared frame against frame after each lighting change.

Honest status

  • Two levels are finished and playable end to end; the story is written past them and the third area is not built.
  • The first competition round is submitted. Judging is under way, and nothing has been awarded.
  • Third-party assets are credited in the build: a museum photogrammetry scan under a non-commercial share-alike licence, several marketplace vegetation and stone packs, and the engine itself. Everything else in the two levels is the team's own work.
  • On one development laptop the frame time spikes periodically regardless of scene, level, frame cap or exclusive fullscreen — it is the machine's hybrid-graphics output path, not the game, and it is documented rather than hidden.
